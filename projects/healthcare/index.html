CARD 01Context

Doctors don't follow fixed schedules.

This was a US-based healthcare startup building an internal platform for hospitals to manage their workforce. The core problem: doctors don't follow fixed schedules. They move between hospitals, work different hours each day, cover emergencies, and their compensation depends on all of that being tracked correctly.

The platform already existed. My job was to improve it: better interface, better accessibility, and cleaner flows for a table that was at the center of everything.

Due to confidentiality, this case study focuses on process and decisions rather than final screens.

CARD 02The challenge

The challenge

When a doctor checks in at a hospital, that information lands in a table. Simple enough. But the table had to handle a lot of variation at the same time: different hospitals with different rules, shifts that changed length or location, emergency coverage, approval states, and automatic hour calculations tied directly to payroll.

How do you make a data-dense table usable for someone who is already under pressure, when every input error has a real financial consequence?

The existing interface had grown over time and it showed. Users had to hold a lot of context in their head just to understand what they were looking at, and it was easy to make mistakes without realizing it.

CARD 03My role

My role

I joined when the research was already done. My focus was on translating those findings into a better interface and validating that it actually worked.

In practice that meant redesigning the table flows and key interactions, reviewing and improving accessibility against WCAG standards, and building interactive prototypes in Axure to test the solutions with real users before anything went to development.

The Axure prototypes were especially important here because the logic was complex. A static mockup could not show how the table behaved when hours changed, or when a shift overlapped with another hospital. People needed to interact with it to find the real problems.

CARD 04Key decisions

Key decisions

Most of the work was about reducing what users had to process at once without removing the information they actually needed.

Progressive disclosure

The table showed a summary view by default. Doctors could see their total hours at a glance, and expand rows only when they needed to review or edit details. Less noise, same data.

Editable vs. locked states

One of the main sources of errors was that users could not tell which fields they could edit and which were locked by an approval. Making that distinction visually clear reduced accidental changes significantly during testing.

Accessibility throughout

The platform needed to work for a wide range of users, including older staff and people using assistive technology. We reviewed contrast, focus states, and keyboard navigation as part of the design process, not as a final checklist.

CARD 05What testing revealed

What testing revealed

The Axure prototypes helped surface things that would have been invisible in a static design. A few examples:

  • Users did not notice validation errors when they appeared inline next to a field. Moving them to a summary at the top of the table made them much harder to miss.
  • The grouping logic for multi-hospital shifts was confusing when hours crossed midnight. We needed a clearer visual separation between days.
  • Some interactions that felt obvious to the team were not obvious at all to users who checked in at multiple hospitals in the same day.

Testing with realistic data, not placeholder content, made a big difference in what we caught.

CARD 06Outcome & impact

Outcome & impact

The new flow brought together when and where each doctor worked, connected it to the hospital time clock, and made it clear when those hours were ready to be paid.

The redesigned time tracking experience enabled healthcare organizations to accurately register working hours across multiple hospitals while reducing manual errors and operational friction.

The system supported complex calculations related to shifts, variable schedules, and compensation, while remaining compliant with accessibility standards and internal workflows.

By improving clarity and structure within a highly constrained environment, the platform became easier to operate for both medical staff and administrative teams.

CARD 07Why it was complex

This was not a simple data entry table.

The experience needed to support:

  • Multiple hospitals operating under different rules
  • Integration with check-in and check-out systems
  • Automatic hour calculations and validations
  • Data consistency for billing and payroll processes
  • Accessibility requirements for a wide range of users

Each design decision had downstream impact on finance, operations, and compliance, making precision and clarity critical.

✦ REFLECTION

Healthcare enterprise work taught me that good UX here is not about making things beautiful or delightful. It is about making sure people do not make mistakes that cost them money or create compliance problems. That changes how you think about every decision.

The Axure prototyping phase was one of the most valuable parts of the project. Complex table logic does not translate well to static designs, and there is no substitute for letting users actually interact with something before you build it.
